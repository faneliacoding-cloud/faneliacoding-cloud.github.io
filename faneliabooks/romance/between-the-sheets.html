Between the Sheets

by Sarah J. Reed

Two strangers. One rainstorm. A night that changes everything.

Chapter One

Stranded

The rain came out of nowhere, the way weather does in the Pacific Northwest — a full-conviction downpour with thirty seconds of warning. Maya made it to the nearest overhang with her jacket over her head and her grocery bag soaked through and her evening plans reduced to rubble.

The overhang belonged to a bookshop. The bookshop had one other occupant — a man about her height in a gray jacket, also watching the rain with an expression of mild philosophical resignation.

"Impressive," he said, gesturing at the sky.

"We don't have weather like this in San Francisco," she said, which was not entirely true but felt true in this moment.

"Visiting?"

"Work trip. Extended."

He nodded. "First time in Portland?"

"Third. I've mostly seen the inside of conference rooms."

He smiled. "Sounds about right."

His name was James. He was there for something in tech — something he described vaguely with the air of someone who'd had to explain it too many times and found it easiest to underexplain. She was in brand strategy, which she described likewise. They were, she thought, both professionally articulate and personally brief.

They waited out the first twenty minutes of the rain in the overhang. Then he said: "There's a bar a block up that has good whiskey and a fire and they won't mind us staying till this passes."

She looked at the rain and then at him and made the calculation that she sometimes made about strangers — whether they were the kind of stranger who became an incident or the kind who became a story.

"One drink," she said.

They stayed for four.

Chapter Two

The One That Follows

The bar had a fire, as promised, and whiskey, and also a very good bartender who had the professional intelligence not to comment on the way the two of them had migrated from the far end of the bar to close proximity over the course of an hour and a half.

They talked the way strangers sometimes talk — with a certain freedom that came from knowing nothing would follow. She told him about her ex, briefly, the one who'd said she worked too hard and then turned out to mean something specific by that. He told her about the last two years, which had involved a cross-country move and a significant reassessment of most of his previous assumptions. They were both, she thought, people who'd arrived at some kind of turning point and weren't sure what to turn toward.

"Do you like Portland?" she asked.

"I'm learning to," he said. "It takes more time than you'd think."

"Most things worth liking do."

He looked at her. "What are you like at home? In your real life."

She thought about it honestly. "More guarded," she said. "Here I'm — I don't know. I'm on a trip. Rules feel different on a trip."

"Is that what this is? A different rules situation?"

"Isn't it?"

He was quiet for a moment, looking into his glass. "What would you want it to be?"

She looked at the fire. Outside the rain still fell, and the city was soft and indistinct through the window, and she was a thousand miles from her ordinary self.

"I don't know yet," she said. "Let's find out."

Chapter Three

Morning

Morning in Portland was gray and clean, rain-washed, with the particular quiet of a city that's recovered from the night before. Maya lay in the low light of the hotel room looking at the ceiling and conducting a brief moral inventory.

She felt: not regret. Something closer to wonder, and also a particular kind of clarity that sometimes follows from doing something unexpected.

James was awake beside her, looking at the same ceiling with what appeared to be a similar quality of thought.

"You okay?" he asked.

"Yes." She meant it. "You?"

"Unexpectedly, yes."

They lay side by side for a moment, which felt both domestic and dissonant with everything — they were, fundamentally, strangers who had shared a rain shower and a bar and a night and nothing else.

"I leave Thursday," she said.

"I know." He'd known since the bar. "I wasn't — this isn't a situation where I'm going to pretend it's something it isn't."

She looked at him. He had, she thought, a very honest face — not in the sense of being uncomplicated, but in the sense that it didn't hide what it felt. She'd trusted it immediately, which had surprised her.

"What is it, then?" she asked.

"Something real," he said. "Brief, but real. Is that enough?"

She thought about how rarely things were simply what they were — how often she waited for more, or held back for more, or let good things pass because they didn't have the right shape.

"Yes," she said. "I think it is."

They ordered room service and stayed in until noon. It was, she decided, enough. More than enough.

Chapter Four

After

She thought about him on the plane home. This was predictable and she'd predicted it.

What she hadn't predicted was the text two weeks later: *I know this is outside the established rules, but I found the book you mentioned. The one about the war photographer. I'm sending it. No agenda.*

The book arrived. It was exactly what she'd described. There was a handwritten note inside: *In case you want to argue about it later. — J*

She called Priya, who had opinions.

"You're going back," Priya said.

"I live here."

"You're going back to Portland."

"I have a meeting there in six weeks." A pause. "Tentatively."

"Maya."

"It complicated what I thought was uncomplicated, okay? These things happen."

"These things happen," Priya repeated, with affectionate skepticism, "to people who want them to happen."

Maya looked at the book on her nightstand. She'd read the first chapter twice. She thought about the rain and the fire and the gray morning ceiling.

"I want to find out if it's different when you both know there's something after," she said. "Not just a night."

"That's either very brave or very foolish."

"Can't it be both?"

"Oh, almost certainly," Priya said. "Go find out."

✦ ✦ ✦
Chapter Five

What We Choose

She went back to Portland on purpose. She told herself it was the meeting. It was the meeting and also the other thing, and she was done pretending these were separate.

He met her at the same bar, which felt like inevitability dressed up as coincidence. The fire was going and the weather wasn't even bad this time — it was a clear October evening — but the bar felt right anyway.

"You came back," he said.

"The city has good whiskey," she said.

He smiled. "That's the only reason?"

She sat down and looked at him — this person she barely knew and somehow also recognized, which was one of the stranger experiences available to human beings.

"I read the book," she said. "You were right about the third chapter."

"I know I was right about the third chapter."

"Don't be smug."

"I'm going to be a little smug."

They stayed for hours again, talking this time about things they hadn't talked about before — her family, his, the specific shape of what they each wanted from the years ahead. These were not the conversations of strangers. They were the conversations of people who had decided, cautiously, to find out what they might become.

When they left the bar, the street was lit with strings of light, and the city looked the way cities look when they're not just backdrop but part of the story.

"So," she said, on the sidewalk.

"So," he agreed.

She looked at him. He looked at her. The night was cold and clear and between them was everything still unmade and waiting.

"I think I'd like to find out," she said.

"Find out what?"

"What this looks like when it takes up more than a night." She paused. "If you want to."

He did. He very much did.

She smiled, for the first time, the kind of smile she didn't manage for strangers. "Then let's find out," she said.

And they did.
