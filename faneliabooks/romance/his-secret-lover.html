His Secret Lover

by Evelyn Reed

Some secrets change you before you ever get to tell them.

Chapter One

Room 714

The hotel bar was the kind of place where anonymity was not an accident but a design philosophy. Low lighting, deep booths, a bartender who had clearly seen everything and had opinions about none of it. Naomi had chosen it specifically because no one from her office would ever come here at 9 PM on a Wednesday after a conference all day.

She had not chosen it because she expected anyone to sit down across from her uninvited.

"Long day?" The man who slid into the seat across the table did it with the specific confidence of someone who didn't often get told no. He was polished — good suit, the kind of watch that told time and also made a point.

"I'm waiting for someone," she said, which was not entirely a lie — she was waiting for the version of herself that still went to bed at a reasonable hour.

"Afraid he's going to be late," he said, with a slight smile. "Since he's me, and I just sat down."

She blinked. "Excuse me?"

"I'm Dominic. I was supposed to meet some colleagues here but they've bailed. You looked like someone who'd rather not drink alone." He tilted his head. "I could be wrong."

"You're usually not wrong," she guessed.

"Almost never." He flagged down the bartender. "What are you having?"

She told herself she would finish the drink and leave. She told herself this firmly and sincerely and with full intention. She was a woman of principles. She had a plan for most things.

She stayed for three hours. They talked about everything and nothing — her work in architecture, his in finance, the conference they were apparently both attending. He was funny without working at it, perceptive without being clinical. When he leaned forward to hear her over the ambient music, she could feel the specific warmth of attention being paid.

When she finally said goodnight, he touched her wrist lightly.

"Room 714," he said. "If you change your mind about anything."

She did not change her mind that night.

But she thought about Room 714 for a very long time.

Chapter Two

Seventeen Days Later

Seventeen days later she was in Chicago for a follow-up meeting with a client, and she checked into her hotel and found a text from a number she hadn't deleted.

*Purely coincidental: I'm also in Chicago. Drinks?*

She knew it was not coincidental. She knew because she had mentioned the Chicago trip during their conversation at the bar, and she knew because Dominic was the kind of man who remembered things and acted on them and then described the result as coincidence with that slight smile.

She went.

What followed was three days in which Naomi learned a number of things: that she had been operating on a kind of emotional autopilot for longer than she'd realized; that she was not, in fact, as contained as she'd believed herself to be; and that Dominic Renard had a way of making her feel fully seen that she hadn't experienced in years.

She also learned what she'd suspected from the beginning: he wore a wedding ring, which he never removed but also never mentioned. A particular kind of oversight that wasn't one.

On the last night, she held his hand and looked at it for a moment.

"You're not going to say anything about it?" he asked.

"What would you like me to say?"

He was quiet. "I don't have an honest answer to that question."

She'd appreciated the honesty, at least. Dishonest men never admitted there were questions they couldn't answer honestly. She found that she was not as angry as she should have been, and she didn't quite know what to do with that.

She went home to Portland. He went home to wherever home was. She thought she'd heard the last of it.

She had not heard the last of it.

Chapter Three

The Weight of What Is Unsaid

He called her the following Tuesday, which was not something she'd expected. She'd half-expected a text — something she could choose the timing of, something she could put down and come back to. A call was present tense. A call required an answer in real time.

She answered.

"I want to be honest with you," he said, without preamble. "My marriage has been over for two years. We're still working out the legal side. I should have told you that."

She sat with this for a moment. It didn't change every part of what had happened, but it changed enough of it to shift the way she was holding things.

"Why didn't you?"

"Because I didn't know what this was. I still don't." A pause. "But I know it's something. And I'm not in the habit of pursuing things I don't intend to take seriously."

Naomi looked out her office window at the skyline. Architecture had taught her to read structures — to understand which walls held weight and which were cosmetic, which foundations were sound and which were settling. She'd spent her career learning to tell the difference.

"I don't do complicated," she said.

"That's funny," he said. "Because for someone who doesn't do complicated, you're remarkably good at it."

She laughed, despite herself. "I hate you a little."

"I know. I find it oddly encouraging."

She told him she'd think about it. She thought about it for the rest of the day. She thought about it over dinner alone, and while doing the dishes, and while lying in bed watching the ceiling. She thought about all the sensible reasons to close this door, and she thought about the way he'd said *I find it oddly encouraging* and meant it.

She called him back at eleven.

"I'm not promising anything," she said.

"I'm not asking for promises," he said. "Just time."

Just time, she thought. She'd see what she could do.

Chapter Four

All the Things We Risk

The thing about time was that it moved, and things happened in it whether you meant them to or not. By February, Naomi had seen Dominic seven times in four months. The coordinates of their relationship were unusual — different cities, hotel lobbies, nights that existed in a separate compartment from her ordinary life.

She'd told her best friend, Priya, who'd raised an eyebrow and said, "This is either a terrible idea or a great one, and I genuinely can't tell which."

"Join the club," Naomi had said.

What she hadn't expected was how much she'd come to rely on his calls. Not the in-person time — that was charged and deliberately separate — but the ordinary calls, the ones that were just about how his day had gone and what book she was reading and whether she'd finally tried the ramen place she'd been mentioning for three weeks. Those had crept up on her, become fixtures in her week that she noticed when they weren't there.

One evening he asked: "Do you think this could be real? If the conditions were different?"

She thought about it carefully, which was the only way she thought about anything. "I think the conditions are always part of the thing," she said. "You can't fully separate what something is from the circumstances that made it what it is."

"That's an architect's answer."

"I'm an architect."

He was quiet for a moment. "The paperwork finalized last week. I thought you should know."

She looked out her window at the winter dark. Outside, the city hummed with the business of ordinary lives.

"Dominic," she said.

"Yeah."

"You should ask me to dinner. A proper dinner. In a city where at least one of us actually lives."

The pause before he answered felt like held breath, like the moment before a building settles into its foundation for good.

"Portland?" he said.

"Portland," she said. "Two weeks. I know a place."

Chapter Five

No Longer Secret

The restaurant she chose was small and well-lit, with tables that weren't hidden and a menu she'd been meaning to try for months. She wore a dress she'd bought for the occasion, which she hadn't done in longer than she could remember.

He was there first, sitting at the table she'd reserved, looking at the menu with the focused attention he brought to most things. He stood when he saw her, and she saw the slight adjustment in his expression — the way it went from composed to something less guarded, something more.

They had dinner. Real dinner. No conference, no lobby, no separate compartment. A Tuesday night in Portland, two people sharing a table and a meal and whatever it was they were choosing to try to become.

"I'm nervous," he said, which she hadn't expected him to admit.

"Good," she said. "So am I."

"That's reassuring somehow."

"People who aren't nervous don't care how things turn out."

He reached across the table and took her hand, and she let him, and they sat for a moment in the comfortable weight of what was real — not a secret, not a compartment, not a story she'd have to explain away. Just this: two people, one table, a city outside, and the careful, hopeful act of beginning something in the light.

It was, she suspected, the harder version. The version where you couldn't disappear when things got difficult, where you had to actually show up. The version she'd been avoiding for reasons she was no longer sure were good enough.

"What are you thinking?" he asked.

She thought about Room 714, and Chicago, and all the conversations in the dark, and the months of holding this thing at arm's length while also, clearly, not letting it go.

"I'm thinking that secrets are a temporary shelter," she said. "Eventually you have to decide whether to build something real."

He looked at her for a long moment, the candle between them making everything warm.

"Then let's build something real," he said.

She raised her glass. He raised his. Outside, Portland went about its evening, entirely unbothered, and inside, quietly, something new began to be.
