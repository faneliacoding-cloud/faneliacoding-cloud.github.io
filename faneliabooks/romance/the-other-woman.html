The Other Woman

by Evelyn St. Clair

The woman in the shadows has a story too.

Chapter One

What I Knew

Let me tell you what I knew: I knew he was married. I knew it from the beginning — from the second evening, when he answered his phone in the bathroom and came back with that specific quality of casual he wore when he was being careful. I recognized it. I'm not innocent.

But let me also tell you what else I knew: I knew that he looked at me like I was the only person in a room full of people, which was something nobody had done in a very long time. I knew that our conversations lasted hours without either of us trying. I knew that I had been quietly waiting to feel like this and had nearly convinced myself I'd waited too long.

So yes. I knew. And I made a choice. I tell you that without apology and without plea for your sympathy. I made a choice with the information I had, and I will account for it.

Her name was Renata. I found out later — not in the beginning, not when I chose. Later, when it mattered differently.

By then I already understood that the relevant question was never *did I know he was married.*

The relevant question was *who did I become during the knowing,* and what I did when that answer arrived.

Chapter Two

The Geography of Us

We met in hotel lobbies and wine bars and once, memorably, in a museum on a Tuesday afternoon, neither of us with a particular reason to be there. We existed in those liminal spaces that belong to no one's real life — spaces that feel temporary and therefore safe. That should have told me something.

He was smart and funny and he asked questions nobody else asked me, which was partly charm and partly genuine curiosity and partly, I think, the freedom that comes from a compartment. In a compartment you can be who you are because the compartment doesn't go anywhere else. You don't have to integrate. You don't have to be consistent. You can just be the best version you remember.

I was the best version of myself, for a while. I was interesting and uncomplicated and I didn't ask things he couldn't answer. I kept the compartment tidy.

And then one day I didn't want to anymore.

I wanted to call him when something funny happened at work. I wanted to say his name at my own dinner table. I wanted to see him on a Sunday afternoon, in ordinary light, without ceremony. These were the things I couldn't want, and the wanting of them was the beginning of the end.

The beginning of the end of what, though? That was what I had to figure out. What, exactly, was ending.

Chapter Three

Her Name

I found out her name from a photograph on his laptop screen — not through snooping, not through anything calculated. He'd stepped out and the screen saver hadn't locked and she was just there: a woman about my age at a dinner table, laughing at something offscreen, unaware of me looking.

I looked for a long time.

She was beautiful in the way that people are beautiful when they're genuinely happy — open, unreserved, caught in a moment she wasn't managing. Her name was in the file name: *renata_birthday.jpg.* Renata. A name like water.

I didn't feel what I expected to feel. I expected something competitive, something cold. What I felt was something closer to shame — not for the first time, but for the first time directly, connected to a face. Renata. Someone's wife. Someone who didn't know about me and had done nothing to deserve knowing.

When he came back I was on the other side of the room.

"You okay?" he asked.

"I need to ask you something," I said. "And I need a true answer."

He sat down. He knew. Men always know when it's that kind of moment.

"What's her name?" I asked.

"Renata," he said.

"Is there any version of this where it ends well?" I asked. "For her."

He was quiet for a long time. Long enough that I had my answer.

"I thought not," I said.

I didn't hate him. I was trying not to even hate myself. But I knew, in that moment, that I was done.

Chapter Four

The Cost

Walking away from something you chose is a particular kind of lonely — different from ordinary loss. With ordinary loss something is taken from you. This was putting something down, which required admitting you'd been carrying something you shouldn't have been. The admission is the part that costs.

I spent six weeks not calling him. He called twice; I let it go to voicemail and didn't listen to them for a week and then listened to them both in one sitting and then deleted them. I don't know what I was afraid of. Being changed my mind, maybe. Being soft in the wrong moment.

My friend Dana came over one evening with wine and the specific expression of a person who already knew the situation and was there to be company rather than conversation.

"How are you?" she asked.

"I made a bad choice," I said. "I'm trying to figure out who I am outside of it."

"That's not a quick project."

"No," I agreed.

"Do you love him?"

I thought about it honestly. "I love the person I was when I was with him," I said. "I'm not sure that's the same."

Dana refilled my glass. "That might be the most important distinction you could make."

I thought about Renata, who didn't know and hadn't chosen, who was at home not knowing. I thought about what we owed each other as women — the strangers we'd never meet, connected by the choices of someone we'd both trusted.

I didn't owe her a confession. But I owed her not perpetuating it.

That was the cost. Not as dramatic as it sounds. Just the quiet putting down of something I'd been pretending didn't weigh what it weighed.

Chapter Five

After the Knowing

Here is what comes after: ordinary life, which is both less and more than what you think it is when you're in a compartment.

I started running again, which I'd stopped doing the year before for reasons I couldn't remember. I picked up a project I'd abandoned. I called my mother more. Small recalibrations, the kind that seem trivial and aren't.

Three months later I met someone at a work event — someone single, someone without the architecture of a hidden life — and we talked for an hour and he asked for my number and I gave it to him and I felt, for the first time in quite a while, something uncomplicated. An attraction without the adjacent guilt. A possibility without the adjacent cost.

I don't tell this story as a redemption arc. I don't think I'm redeemed. I think I'm accountable, which is different. Redemption is granted. Accountability is taken.

What I know now that I didn't know then: the compartment is not safe. The compartment is just postponement — of what's real, what you owe, who you're becoming. Eventually the compartment runs out of air and you have to decide what you're willing to breathe.

I chose to come out.

Renata never knew my name. I don't know if that's mercy or just logistics. But somewhere she's living her life, unknown to me, and that life belongs to her.

And mine belongs to me. The version of me that looked at that photograph and made a different choice.

That's the version I'm trying to be.
