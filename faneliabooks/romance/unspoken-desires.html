Unspoken Desires

by Isabella Ward

Some rules exist to be tested. This is one of them.

Chapter One

Floor Twenty-Seven

The policy at Kensington & Park was clear on the subject: no relationships between employees on the same project team. It said so in the handbook on page forty-three, under Professional Conduct, in language Adrienne Kwan had helped revise the previous year. She'd thought it was reasonable then. She thought it was reasonable now. She thought it was reasonable and also, standing in the elevator on a Monday morning with Nathan Park, she thought of it not at all.

He was the new VP of Strategy, transferred from the Chicago office. He had a reputation for precise thinking and asking difficult questions and not being interested in anything that wasn't honest. These were things Adrienne valued. She had not, until he moved into the office two floors up and appeared on her project team, experienced these as complications.

The elevator was empty except for the two of them. She was looking at her phone. He was looking at nothing in particular in the specific way of someone who'd just decided not to look at something.

"Late night?" he asked.

"Is it visible?"

"Only to people who pay attention."

She looked at him then. He met her eyes with the same equanimity he brought to twenty-person presentations.

"Do you?" she asked. "Pay attention."

"Generally," he said. "Selectively, more recently."

The elevator opened at her floor. She got out. She walked to her office and opened her laptop and opened the project file and thought, with great discipline, only about the project.

She thought about him twice before lunch.

Chapter Two

Across the Table

They were on the same deliverable for six weeks — an analysis that required something close to collaboration, which meant meetings and shared documents and, twice, working late in the same conference room with everyone else gone.

She was professional. She was the most professional she'd been in her career. She used his last name in meetings. She sent concise emails. She responded to everything in under twenty-four hours, which was efficient and also, she acknowledged, a symptom.

He was equally professional. He addressed her by name, which she noticed, and gave her credit for ideas, which she valued, and once — once — after a particularly good piece of analysis she'd caught that he hadn't, he'd said, quietly, so only she heard: "That was excellent work. How did you see that?"

The question had more respect in it than she was used to. She'd answered directly, without the modest deflection she usually deployed for compliments. He'd listened with his full attention, nodded, said "of course," and moved on.

She told her friend Mei about it that evening, framed carefully.

"He complimented your work," Mei said.

"He asked a genuine follow-up question, which is—"

"Adrienne."

"What?"

"You are describing a workplace interaction with the specificity of someone who has memorized it."

She had, in fact, not memorized it. She could simply recall it with great accuracy, which was different.

"We're on the same project," she said.

"Sure," said Mei, with a precision of tone that deserved its own professional commendation.

Chapter Three

The Meeting After the Meeting

The project closed in October. Final presentation, good outcome, the client pleased and the team celebrating in a predictable way at the bar around the corner. Adrienne had two glasses of wine and left at a reasonable hour, which was sensible and also a specific kind of avoidance she recognized but couldn't fully articulate.

The next morning, Nathan knocked on her office door.

"The project's over," he said, from the doorway.

"I'm aware," she said.

"Which means we're no longer on the same team."

She looked at him. "We still work in the same company."

"Different floor. Different project lead." He stepped into the office and closed the door behind him, which was a thing he had never done, and her awareness of the room's dimensions adjusted accordingly. "I want to say something and I want to say it directly, because I think you'll prefer it that way."

"Generally," she said, because saying *yes* might have done something to her voice.

"I've been managing a situation for six weeks that I think you've also been managing. I've done it because the situation called for management and I didn't want to put you in a position. The situation has changed."

She looked at him across her desk. "What would you like to say, Nathan?"

"I'd like to ask," he said, with the clarity he brought to everything, "whether you'd want to have dinner. Not professionally."

The answer was immediate. She'd known it for six weeks. "Yes," she said.

He nodded, the way he nodded when he got what he was looking for. "Friday," he said. "I'll make a reservation."

"You already have one," she guessed.

"Wednesday, in case you said no. I moved it." A slight, genuine smile. "The only thing I wasn't certain about."

She looked at him — this man who thought carefully and moved precisely and asked directly for what he wanted — and felt, for the first time in professional memory, the very pleasant experience of being matched.

"Friday," she said.

He left. She sat very still for thirty seconds and then, with great discipline, opened her laptop and worked productively for the remaining hours of the day.

She remembered not a single word of it.

Chapter Four

What Uncertainty Looks Like

Dinner was easy. That surprised her — she'd expected the first dinner to be the careful version, the one where you're both performing composure. It wasn't. It was two and a half hours that felt like forty minutes, food she'd describe later with difficulty because she'd been paying attention to other things.

He asked about her work before Kensington. She told him. He told her about Chicago, about what he'd wanted from the transfer and what it had given him instead, about the years of working toward something and the recent discovery that the thing he'd been working toward was not quite the thing he'd wanted. She recognized this — had experienced her own version of it — and said so.

"What do you actually want?" he asked.

"This year or longitudinally?"

"Pick one."

She thought about it. "To be known," she said. "Not by everyone. By a few people who see me properly. The real version."

"I think I've seen the real version," he said.

She looked at him over her glass. "You've seen the professional version. That's well-developed but it's not the same."

"Tell me the difference."

So she did. In a restaurant on a Friday evening, for the first time in a long time, she told someone the difference. He listened with the same quality of attention he brought to his work, which was the quality of someone for whom listening was also understanding.

Walking out, on the sidewalk, he said: "Thank you for telling me that."

"You asked."

"A lot of people ask," he said. "Not everyone gets an actual answer."

She stood on the sidewalk looking up at him and thought: this is the real version. This, right here, is the person I am when something is worth being real for.

"Nathan," she said.

"Yeah."

"I liked this," she said. "I want to do it again."

He smiled — the non-professional one that she'd been tracking alongside the professional one, cataloging the differences. "I know a place on Tuesday," he said. "Better than this one."

"How do you know it's better?"

"I've been waiting for the right occasion."

She looked at him for a moment, this precise, direct, quietly particular man, and felt the specific gravity of wanting something real.

"Tuesday," she said.

It was better.

Chapter Five

What Was Always There

Six months later, they told HR. This was, per Adrienne's research, the correct sequence: not concurrent with the relationship, not after it had become impossible to conceal, but at the point of seriousness. She'd drafted the conversation outline on a legal pad. Nathan had reviewed it and suggested one edit.

"You can't submit an outline for an HR conversation," he said.

"It's structural," she said. "For clarity."

"It's structured to the point of being a brief."

"Thank you."

He looked at the ceiling for a moment. "I mean it affectionately."

She kept the outline.

The conversation went smoothly, as prepared conversations tend to. HR noted the disclosure, flagged the project reporting structure to avoid any future conflict, and moved on. The woman in HR had, Adrienne suspected, seen this a hundred times and had opinions about none of it.

After, walking back through the lobby, Nathan said: "That was anticlimactic."

"I prefer anticlimactic," she said.

"I know you do." He took her hand in the lobby, which was entirely visible to anyone who happened to look, and she noted this without moving away. "No more managing," he said.

"No more managing," she agreed.

They walked to the elevator. It was a Monday morning. She was exactly where she worked, with exactly the person she'd spent six weeks not looking at across a conference table. Nothing about it was covert or careful or contained.

It felt, she thought, like the end of a very long negotiation in which both parties had finally reached agreement.

Which was, given who they were, exactly the right way to arrive here.
