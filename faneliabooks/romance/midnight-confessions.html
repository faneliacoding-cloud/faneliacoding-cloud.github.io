Midnight Confessions

by Clara Elias

Some conversations are best had in the dark.

Chapter One

11:47 PM

It started with a wrong number. At 11:47 PM on a Thursday, Jade's phone buzzed with a text from a number she didn't recognize: *Are you still awake? I can't stop thinking about the Barcelona thing.*

She stared at it. She had not been to Barcelona. She had not left the tristate area in fourteen months.

She typed: *Wrong number. But also, what's the Barcelona thing?*

There was a pause — thirty seconds, long enough for her to regret it. Then: *You're right, wrong number. Sorry to bother.*

She should have left it there. She absolutely should have left it there. She was thirty-one years old and a reasonably sensible person and she knew the general principle that you did not engage with wrong numbers at midnight.

She typed: *You don't have to tell me. But now I'm curious.*

Another pause. Longer this time.

*It's something I said in Barcelona that I've been thinking about ever since. To someone who probably forgot it immediately.*

*What did you say?*

*That I thought I'd been taking my life too seriously and not seriously enough at the same time.*

Jade put her phone down and then picked it up again. She was in her kitchen in her pajamas, and she had work in the morning, and she was absolutely going to respond.

*That sounds like the right thing to think in Barcelona,* she wrote.

*It sounds like the right thing to think anywhere,* he wrote back.

They talked until 2 AM. She never asked his name.

Chapter Two

The Rules of the Dark

They established, without quite deciding to, a set of patterns. He texted late — never before ten, rarely before eleven — and she answered, because she'd discovered that she slept badly and the lateness was actually better for her than she'd ever admitted to anyone. The dark hours had always been when she was most herself.

His name was Marcus. He was in finance. He lived in the same city, which felt simultaneously like a lot and like nothing. He'd just ended a relationship and was in the particular state of post-relationship clarity where you knew exactly what you'd done wrong.

She was between things in a way she found difficult to articulate. Not between relationships exactly — she'd been single long enough that it had become simply her state rather than a transitional one. Between versions of herself, maybe. The one who'd spent her twenties wanting a specific kind of life and the one still deciding what the next version wanted.

He asked good questions. Not the getting-to-know-you questions but the getting-to-know-what-someone-actually-thought questions. What kept her up at night, and what got her out of bed, and what she'd do if she weren't doing what she was doing.

*Do you always talk like this to wrong numbers?* she asked once.

*Only the interesting ones,* he said.

*How do you know I'm interesting?*

*You told me what you're afraid of on the fourth night. Interesting people know their fears by name.*

✦ ✦ ✦
Chapter Three

The Complications of Knowing

Three weeks in, she knew more about Marcus than she knew about most people she'd known for years. She knew he made coffee before he could think in the morning and listened to jazz when he worked and called his mother every Sunday, not only when things were good but when things were bad, which she found more telling than almost anything else.

She also knew that they'd never seen each other, and that this fact, which should have been strange, felt mostly like a feature. There was a specific freedom in being known without being seen — the freedom of the text, the dark, the permission to be thoughtful without being watched.

But one night he asked: *Do you ever wonder if this would be different in person?*

She'd been lying in bed with the lights off, phone on her chest, and she looked at the question for a while.

*Yes,* she wrote. *I think it would be different. I don't know if different means better or worse.*

*Different how?*

*There's less room to perform well in person. You see things you can't unsee. It's harder.*

*Is harder bad?*

She thought about it. *No,* she wrote finally. *I think I've been avoiding harder for a while.*

He didn't respond immediately. When he did, it was: *Me too.*

The word sat between them, three letters and a great deal of weight.

✦ ✦ ✦
Chapter Four

Saturday Morning

It was his idea, which she appreciated — that he asked rather than assumed, and asked directly without pretending it wasn't what it was.

*Coffee? Saturday. Public place, obvious exit, you can leave if I turn out to be terrible.*

She said yes. She said yes because she was tired of the dark being the only place she was real, and because she'd told him things she hadn't told anyone, and because if she was honest — and she was, in the dark, always honest — she wanted to see him.

He was already there. She recognized him before she knew his face, which was strange — something about the way he sat, which matched the quality she'd been listening to for weeks. He looked up when she walked in and she watched the same recognition cross his expression.

"You look like yourself," he said, which was one of the stranger compliments she'd received and also exactly right.

They talked for three hours, which felt like both longer and shorter than it was. The coffee shop version of them was different — slightly more careful, slightly less immediate — but the same underneath. The same questions. The same attention.

Walking out, he said: "This was good. But I miss the dark."

"We have it every night," she said.

He smiled. "Yeah. But now I know what you look like when you're saying it."

She thought about that on the way home, and decided it was a net positive.

✦ ✦ ✦
Chapter Five

After Midnight

The texts didn't stop. If anything, they shifted — became more textured, because now they were laid over a face, a voice, a person with weight and presence. She'd find herself mid-conversation thinking: *I know how this sounds when he says it.* The abstraction had become someone real.

Two months after the wrong number, he texted her at his usual hour and asked: *Would you want to try going somewhere that takes more than two hours?*

She was in her kitchen again, in her pajamas again, at almost exactly the same time as the first night. The whole thing had come full circle, and she was aware of it.

*Barcelona?* she wrote.

*Too on the nose,* he sent back.

She laughed alone in her kitchen and felt, clearly and simply, happy — the low-key, genuine kind that didn't need an occasion.

*Anywhere,* she wrote. *Pick somewhere.*

He sent her a link: a small town on the coast, a long weekend, some kind of autumn festival involving food he described as "possibly excellent, possibly a disaster, worth finding out."

She looked at the link. She thought about the dark and the conversations and the particular way this had built — slowly, in the quiet hours, out of a mistake that turned out not to be one.

*Book it,* she wrote. *I'll pack for possibly excellent.*

His response came immediately, which meant he'd been holding his phone, waiting.

*Already did,* he said. *Three weeks ago.*

She put her phone down and laughed to herself in the good way and went to make tea, and outside the city went its insomniac way, and somewhere across it Marcus was awake at midnight, which was when he was most himself, and she was too, and they both knew it now.

Some conversations are best had in the dark. Some lives, it turned out, are best built there too.
