Second Chances

by Sarah Jenkins

Some loves are worth a second read.

Chapter One

Eleven Years

It had been eleven years. Elena counted without meaning to — the way you count anything you've been trying not to count — standing in the coffee shop on the corner of Morrison and Fifth, waiting for her order, when she looked up and saw David Lau walking through the door.

Eleven years. He looked the same in the ways that mattered and different in the ways that everything gets different. A few lines. A more deliberate way of carrying himself. The gray at his temples, which she could not decide how she felt about, because *distinguished* was not a word she'd meant to be applying.

He saw her at the same moment she saw him. The pause was brief — just a breath of recalibration — and then he crossed the room.

"Elena."

"David." She picked up her coffee because having something to hold made everything marginally more manageable. "I didn't know you were back in Portland."

"Last month. My firm opened an office here."

"That's—" She searched for a word that was neither too warm nor too flat. "That's great. How long?"

"Indefinitely." He looked at her with the particular attention he'd always given her, which had felt like being properly seen and which she'd been reminding herself for eleven years was just a thing he did, probably, with most people. "Do you have a minute?"

She did not have a minute. She had a full day of no minutes. She said: "Sure."

Chapter Two

What We Were

They sat in the window and she learned eleven years of his life in forty minutes. A marriage — to a woman named Jess, a graphic designer — that had ended three years ago, mutually and without drama, two people who had been better friends than partners and had finally admitted it. No children, by choice then and by circumstance later. A lot of work. A period in Barcelona that he'd described as "clarifying." He'd come back changed in some way he was still unpacking.

She told him her version. The gallery she'd opened and closed and the different gallery she'd opened that had survived. The years of living alone that she'd been afraid of and that had turned out to be important. The relationship that had lasted two years and ended with mutual respect and the specific sadness of outliving something that had been good but hadn't been quite right.

"Are you happy?" he asked.

It was the kind of question only someone who'd known you a long time could ask that way — not as a pleasantry but as a real inquiry.

"Mostly," she said. "More than I used to be. You?"

"I'm working on it." He turned his cup in his hands. "Honestly, I'm trying to figure out what I actually want from things. I think I spent a long time wanting what made sense on paper."

She knew that feeling. She knew it with the specific recognition of someone who'd done the same thing.

"It takes longer than you'd think," she said.

"To figure it out?"

"To stop wanting the paper version and start wanting the real one."

He looked at her across the table, and the eleven years contracted briefly to nothing, and she felt the same thing she'd felt in a different coffee shop in a different phase of their lives: the particular warmth of being in the right place.

She didn't say it. But she thought it.

Chapter Three

Trying Again

They started slowly. Coffee became dinner became walks became the natural rhythm of people relearning each other — cautiously, because they both knew what the cost of this going wrong would be.

Her friend Mae said: "I never understood why you two didn't work the first time."

"We were twenty-four," Elena said. "The timing was wrong."

"And now the timing is right?"

Elena thought about it. "Now we're different people. That's not the same as the timing being right. It might be better."

She was being careful. She knew she was being careful. After eleven years you developed scar tissue in specific places, and she could feel the places where hers was — the moments where she'd reach for connection and feel herself pull back just before making contact, the reflexive self-protection that had kept her safe and also, occasionally, kept her alone.

David was careful too, and in the same ways, which was either a problem or a gift and she hadn't decided which.

One evening, walking along the waterfront, he said: "Can I ask you something I should have asked eleven years ago?"

She looked at him. "Probably."

"What did you need, then, that you weren't getting?"

She was quiet for a while. The river moved. The lights on the bridges made long reflections.

"Space to be uncertain," she said finally. "I needed to not have everything decided. I think I was afraid that if I stayed, I'd spend my whole life being just one thing, and I didn't know yet what all my things were."

He nodded slowly. "I needed you to ask for that. I didn't know how to offer it."

"We both failed the communication portion," she said.

He laughed, surprising her. "Comprehensively."

She laughed too. They walked, and the river went its way, and she thought that laughing about a failure was a good sign for whether you could do better the second time.

Chapter Four

Risk It

Six weeks in, he cooked her dinner. This felt significant — not the cooking, but the admission of domestic intention, the willingness to be seen in an ordinary context, not just the curated context of restaurants and walks.

His apartment was organized in the way of people who live alone and have learned exactly how much disorder they can tolerate. Books in a particular order. Photographs on a single wall. A kitchen that was functional and not decorative, used.

They ate at the small table by the window and he'd made something Thai — not adventurously, not to impress, just because he'd been cooking it for years and it was good. She found that she liked knowing he'd been cooking this for years, this ordinary thing, this regular comfort. It was a window into the eleven years of his ordinary life.

"I'm scared," she said, over dessert. She hadn't planned to say it.

"Of what specifically?" Not dismissive. Genuinely asking.

"Of doing this and it going the same way."

"It won't go the same way," he said. "We're not the same people."

"That's not guaranteed to make it better."

"No," he agreed. "But the things that went wrong before were about what we were missing. We know what we were missing. That's worth something."

She looked at him across the small table, the city outside the window, the ordinary Tuesday evening, and she thought: this is the version where you either close the door permanently or you open it all the way.

"I want to try," she said. "Really try. Not carefully."

He reached across the table. "Not carefully," he agreed.

She took his hand. It was warm and familiar and new all at once. That was the only way she knew how to describe it.

That was enough.

Chapter Five

The Second Read

Her gallery showed a photography series in March — a collection about what places looked like in winter versus spring, the same locations photographed twice. How permanence hid behind the seasonal. How the essential shape of things didn't change even when everything around them did.

David came to the opening. He wore a jacket she hadn't seen before and held a glass of sparkling water and stood in front of her favorite photograph in the series for a long time before she joined him.

"The tree in this one," he said. "It's the exact same tree. You can tell by the branch structure."

"That's the point," she said.

He looked at her. "You picked this series for a reason."

She smiled. "Maybe."

Outside, the March evening was hesitantly warm, the first real warmth of the year, and people on the sidewalk looked slightly surprised by it, the way you always were — each year somehow forgetting that it came back.

They walked home after. His hand found hers on the sidewalk and she didn't pull away and didn't brace and didn't perform casualness about it. She just walked, with her hand in his, through the warming March night, and thought about the tree in winter and the tree in spring and how the branch structure was the same.

Eleven years, she thought. And still the same essential shape.

"I'm glad you walked into the coffee shop," she said.

"I saw you through the window," he admitted. "I almost didn't come in."

She stopped on the sidewalk and looked at him. "What changed your mind?"

He looked at her for a moment with that attention she'd spent eleven years trying to minimize.

"You looked like someone I'd regret not talking to," he said.

She squeezed his hand and they kept walking, through the March evening, toward wherever this went next.

Toward, she thought, herself. Finally, entirely herself. And him, beside her, reading the second version.
