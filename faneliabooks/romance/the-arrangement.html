The Arrangement

by Evelyn Rose

What starts as pretend doesn't always know how to stop.

Chapter One

The Proposal

Vivienne did not like surprises. She liked plans, and outcomes, and the specific satisfaction of completing a checklist. She was thirty-three years old, a corporate attorney at a firm that valued precision, and she had not once in her professional life improvised on a deadline.

What Dominic Ashworth was proposing was, by any measure, a substantial improvisation.

"You need a fiancée for the Mercer dinner," she said, to confirm she'd understood.

"For the quarter, technically. The Mercer account requires a partner. He's traditional."

"And I get—"

"Access to the Whitmore contacts. Forty names, as agreed."

She looked at him across the conference table. They had been professional acquaintances for two years — opposing counsel on two cases, collegial rather than close. She knew what he looked like in court and in negotiation. She did not know what he looked like in most other contexts, which was relevant.

"You realize this requires a degree of coordination," she said.

"I'm capable of coordination."

"You'll need to know things. My background. My habits. Things a fiancée would know."

He met her eyes. "I learn quickly."

She looked at her legal pad, on which she'd been taking notes as he spoke. She reviewed them now: duration (twelve weeks), appearances (four events), specifics (professional, controlled, no complications).

"No complications," she repeated.

"None," he agreed.

She picked up her pen. "Then we should probably start with the terms."

The terms took forty minutes. She told herself that was a good sign — that someone she could negotiate with was someone she could endure.

She did not tell herself what she actually thought, which was that he'd looked at her twice during the entire discussion with an expression that was not quite professional.

She had noted it, and set it aside.

Chapter Two

Playing the Part

The first event was a charity gala. Vivienne wore a dress she'd owned for two years and never worn — emerald silk, almost theatrical — and when Dominic arrived at the car, he paused briefly on the sidewalk in the way of someone updating a calculation.

"You look—" he started.

"Appropriate for the occasion," she supplied.

"I was going to say good."

"That works too."

They'd prepared. Two dinners' worth of backstory — how they'd met (through mutual friends), when they'd gotten engaged (four months ago, after a trip upstate), what they disagreed about (she said his punctuality habits, he said her territorial relationship with the good pen in the conference room). It was crisp and specific and held up well for a first outing.

What held up less well was the incidentals. The way he guided her through a crowd with his hand at the small of her back — practiced, not quite automatic. The moment they had to recall the same memory for two different people and found, mid-story, that they'd invented something that felt more real than it should have.

"You're good at this," he said, during a quiet moment by the window.

"I'm good at performance," she said. "It's not that different from court."

"Is everything analogous to court for you?"

"Most things," she said, and then thought about it and said, "probably fewer things than I pretend."

He looked at her with that not-quite-professional expression. She had been cataloging them. Three times now.

"Vivienne," he said.

"We should get back," she said, and they did.

Chapter Three

Complications

It was the fourth event, a private dinner at the Mercer estate, that caused the trouble. Small table — eight people, four hours, the particular intensity of close quarters and good wine and the expectation of realness.

They were very good by then. Four weeks in, they moved together with the ease of people who genuinely shared a life — finishing the relevant thoughts, touching each other with the casualness of muscle memory, sustaining the subtle architecture of two individuals making something together. Vivienne had, she acknowledged privately, become quite good at believing the thing she was performing.

After dinner, in the car home, she said: "Mercer seems sold."

"He is." A pause. "You were remarkable tonight."

"We were remarkable. It was a joint effort."

"It was. But you—" He stopped.

"What?"

He looked out the window at the night city. "It's getting harder to remember which part is arrangement and which part isn't."

She went very still. This was the complication she had specifically contracted against. This was the item explicitly excluded from the terms.

"Dominic," she said.

"I know," he said. "I know. Ignore it."

She looked at the city. Four weeks left.

She was beginning to think, uncomfortably, that the issue was not a memory problem. The issue was that the line she'd drawn with such precision was starting to look more like a suggestion.

Chapter Four

Against the Terms

She called a meeting. Her apartment, coffee, a legal pad. She was nothing if not thorough.

"I think we need to revisit the terms," she said.

He sat across from her in her living room, which he'd been in exactly twice and which she'd observed him taking in both times with an attention that felt like interest rather than just data collection.

"Which terms specifically?"

"The no complications clause."

He was quiet. "Are there complications?"

"I believe there may be," she said, with the precision of someone choosing words very carefully. "I want to assess them before they become—" She stopped. "Before they become more than we've planned for."

He leaned forward, elbows on his knees, looking at her in the way he'd been looking at her with increasing frequency — not the professional look, not the performance look, but the one she'd been cataloging and could no longer pretend was incidental.

"What would you want," he asked, "if there were no terms?"

"This is a hypothetical?"

"It is not a hypothetical."

She put the legal pad down. This was, she thought, the negotiation she'd been preparing for without knowing it.

"I don't want a performance," she said. "I've spent twelve weeks performing and I'm very good at it and I don't want to do it with someone I might actually—" She stopped again.

"With someone you might actually what?" he said, with a specificity she recognized as its own kind of precision.

"Trust," she said. "Care about. Both."

He stood up and came around the coffee table and sat next to her on the couch and looked at her with an attention so focused it was nearly unbearable.

"Then let's revise the terms," he said.

Chapter Five

New Clauses

The revised terms were not written down, which was the first indication of how different they were from the original.

They were, as terms go, very simple: honest, present, no performance. Everything they'd called a complication, now renegotiated as the point.

The Mercer account closed in February. By then they'd been to three additional dinners — none of them arranged, all of them real — and the backstory they'd invented had been gradually replaced by actual history: the argument about takeout, the Sunday morning in her apartment reading separate books on the same couch, the conversations that ran past midnight because neither of them wanted to stop.

She'd told him about her sister, who lived far away and who she missed more than she talked about. He'd told her about the year after his father died, when he'd relocated twice and worked dangerously hard to avoid being still. These were not the things you told someone when you were pretending.

"I have a question," she said, one evening in his kitchen while he made something that was optimistically described as pasta.

"Legally or personally?"

"Personally."

He looked at her over his shoulder. "Go ahead."

"Would you have asked me, if there hadn't been an arrangement? If we'd just been two people who knew each other."

He was quiet for a moment. "No," he said.

She'd expected that. "Why?"

"Because you're intimidating. And I was afraid."

"Dominic Ashworth, afraid."

"Of things that matter, yes." He put the spoon down and turned around fully. "Would you have said yes?"

She thought about it properly. "No," she said. "I'd have found a reason not to. I was better at not trying than you'd think."

He looked at her. "So we needed the terms."

"Apparently."

"And now?"

She looked around the kitchen — at him, at the improbable pasta, at seven months of what had started as calculation and arrived here, in this warm ordinary room, as something she couldn't have engineered.

"Now," she said, "I don't need the terms. Take that however you'd like."

He took it correctly. She had known he would.

Outside, the arrangement between them dissolved quietly into something it had been pretending not to be for quite some time, and both of them found, without surprise, that this — the real version, the unwritten version, the one with no clauses and no expiration — fit considerably better than anything they'd planned.
