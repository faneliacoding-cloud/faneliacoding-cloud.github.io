After the Wedding

by Elaine St. Claire

Marriage is not the ending. It is the beginning of everything harder and better.

Chapter One

The First Morning

The wedding had been beautiful. Everyone said so, and everyone was right — the venue, the flowers, the light through the windows at the moment they exchanged vows, all of it exactly as she'd imagined and exactly as it had been planned. Lena was not a woman who left things to chance.

But the first morning — the actual first morning of the marriage, waking in the hotel suite in the gray seven-o'clock light — was something she hadn't planned and couldn't have.

She was awake before Daniel. She lay looking at the ceiling and listening to the unfamiliar rhythm of his sleeping breath, which she'd heard many times but which meant something different here, in this particular morning that was the first of a specific kind of morning that would, theoretically, continue for the rest of her life.

This was not a bad thought. It was not a panicked thought. It was simply a thought that was larger than most of her thoughts, and she was taking it in.

Daniel woke. He looked at her. He said: "You've been awake."

"For a bit."

"Thinking?"

"It's very quiet," she said. "I was just — listening. To the quiet."

He looked at her in the way he'd been looking at her for four years, which was the way of someone who knew when she was managing something and was waiting to find out what.

"This is real," he said.

"I know it's real."

"I mean just — it's real. We did it."

She looked at him in the gray light of the first morning. She thought about all the things they'd built and argued and worked through and planned — the years that had made them stable enough to be here, to have made this specific choice.

"We did it," she said.

He put his arm around her. She lay back down. And the first morning continued in the simple and irreversible way of all mornings, and she thought: this is what I was waiting for. Not the wedding. This.

Chapter Two

Real Life

Real life arrived without ceremony. In retrospect she'd expected it to announce itself somehow — a sense of arrival, of the new chapter beginning marked. Instead it arrived as a Tuesday morning, Daniel's alarm, her coffee, the commute, the meeting she'd been avoiding, dinner made from whatever was in the fridge.

"This is it?" she said, not unhappily, on a Thursday evening four weeks in.

"Tuesday through Sunday?" he said. "Yeah."

"We spent two years planning an occasion and now we're just—"

"Living," he said.

She thought about it. She'd spent a great deal of her life planning occasions. Work, travel, the wedding itself, the specific architecture of what came before the life. And now she was in the life.

"I think I'm better at the before," she admitted.

He looked up from what he was reading. "How so?"

"I know how to work toward things. I'm less practiced at just — being in them."

He set the book down. He had the expression of someone who'd been waiting to have this conversation and was glad it had arrived.

"Me too," he said. "I've been thinking about this too."

"You have?"

"We're both very good at goals," he said. "At pursuit. I think we need to get better at the other thing. The sitting-still thing."

She thought about their courtship, their engagement, their wedding — each stage purposeful, forward-facing. She hadn't been sitting still in four years.

"How do you get better at sitting still?" she asked.

"Practice," he said, and picked his book back up, and they spent the remainder of the evening in comfortable quiet, which was, she thought, probably a start.

Chapter Three

The First Fight That Mattered

They'd argued before. In four years of being together they'd had their share of disagreements — about travel plans and priorities and the specific mechanics of how things should be done, on which she had views. But the first fight of the marriage was different in kind, and she knew it while it was happening.

It was about the apartment, ostensibly — his tendency to leave things unfinished, her tendency to take over before anything could fail. But underneath it was about something older: whose life this was, who was managing what, whether management was the same as control.

She said: "I need things done. We agreed on how things would be handled."

He said: "You need things done *your* way, which you've decided is just the way things work."

Silence. The particular silence of something true said aloud for the first time.

She said, after a while: "That's not fair."

He said: "Which part?"

She could not immediately answer.

They separated for the evening — him on a call, her reading with the door not quite closed, the apartment full of the specific pressure of things unsaid and things too-well-said. Before bed she knocked on the study door.

"The second part," she said. "The part about deciding. That was fair."

He looked at her. "I know it's not how you mean it," he said. "I know you're not doing it on purpose."

"That might be worse," she said.

He came around the desk. He was still, sometimes, the most patient person she knew.

"Then that's what we work on," he said.

She nodded. She leaned her head against his shoulder and stood there for a moment in the study where they'd both been separately difficult, and thought: this is also what marriage is. The after-the-fight moment. The coming back.

She'd practice that too.

Chapter Four

Growing Sideways

The second year of marriage was different from the first. Less careful, more honest — because the first year you were still wearing the marriage like something new, something you could feel on you, and the second year it was just yours, easy in the way of things that fit.

She started a project she'd been putting off for three years, a studio practice she'd abandoned for reasons she could no longer fully remember. He joined a hiking group on Wednesdays, which she'd teased him about and then stopped teasing him about when she saw what it gave him — a tiredness that was different from work tiredness, something physical and satisfying.

They were, she realized, growing — not together exactly, which implied a merged trajectory, but alongside each other. Parallel but connected. Sideways growth, like trees that expand their canopy while staying rooted.

"I think you're happier," he said, watching her come back from the studio one evening with paint on her forearm she hadn't noticed.

"I think we're both happier," she said.

"What changed?"

She thought about the first year and the learning-to-sit-still conversation and the fight about control and all the small adjustments that looked like nothing individually and added up to something real.

"We got better at it," she said simply. "At being married."

He smiled. "Was there a learning curve?"

"Somewhat."

"Worth it?"

She looked at him — this person she'd chosen and who'd chosen her and with whom she'd built something she hadn't known how to build when they'd started. The something that wasn't in the wedding but was in all the Tuesdays.

"Every part," she said.

Chapter Five

What We Made

On their fifth anniversary they went back to the hotel — the same suite, the same city, deliberately. She'd suggested it and he'd agreed with the enthusiasm of someone who'd been thinking about it too.

They had dinner in the same restaurant where they'd celebrated the night before the wedding, and the food was the same and the light was the same and they were not the same at all.

She was thirty-seven and still very good at planning things and working in the studio three mornings a week and also, now, sitting still — not excellent at it, but better. He was thirty-nine and still someone who left things unfinished and who she'd stopped finishing for him and who'd mostly, eventually, finished them himself.

They were, in aggregate, better than when they'd started.

"What do you want from the next five years?" he asked, over dessert.

"Sideways growth," she said, and he laughed because he knew what she meant.

"What else?"

She thought about the question properly. "More of this," she said. "More ordinary Tuesdays. More practice sitting still. More of the things we got better at."

He reached across the table and took her hand. Same gesture, five years later, and it still held the same warmth — specific, familiar, chosen.

"I want to keep showing you things," he said. "And having you show me things."

She thought about the studio and the hiking group and the fight in the study and the morning five years ago when she'd lain in the gray light listening to the quiet. She thought about all the things she hadn't planned and couldn't have and that had turned out to be the ones that mattered.

"Deal," she said.

Outside the window, the city went its ordinary way — busy and indifferent to the small private history being made at Table 4 — and she sat with her hand in his and thought: this is what we made. Not the occasion. The everything after.

The real thing.

It was, she still believed, exactly what she'd been waiting for.
