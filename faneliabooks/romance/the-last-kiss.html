The Last Kiss

by Eliza Reed

Some loves never truly end — they just wait.

Chapter One

The Return

Sofia hadn't planned on running into Marcus at the farmers market on a Tuesday. She'd planned on buying heirloom tomatoes and getting home before noon. She had not planned on dropping her entire canvas bag of groceries when she turned the corner and walked directly into the broad chest of the man she'd loved — and lost — a decade ago.

He caught the bag before it hit the ground. Of course he did. Marcus had always had quick hands.

"Sofia." His voice was the same. That low, steady timbre she'd spent years trying to forget. He straightened and looked at her, and she noticed the lines that hadn't been there before — faint creases at the corners of his eyes, a thread of silver at his temple. He looked distinguished. He looked older. He still looked like him.

"Marcus." She took her bag back. "What are you doing here? I thought you were in Seattle."

"I moved back. Three months ago." He paused. "Mom's health."

"Oh." Something shifted in her chest — not quite sympathy, not quite grief, but something in that neighborhood. "I'm sorry. How is she?"

"Manageable. Stubborn as ever." A ghost of a smile. "You'd recognize the type."

Sofia blinked. Was that a joke? Was he allowed to make jokes with her? She hadn't established the rules yet for this encounter, mostly because she'd never imagined it was an encounter she'd have to have.

"I should go," she said, because it was true and also because she could feel her composure starting to slip at the edges, like a wet paper bag. "It was good to see you."

"Was it?" He wasn't being cruel. He was being honest. Marcus had always been honest, even when she wished he wasn't.

She didn't answer. She turned and walked away, and she didn't let herself look back, even though every single part of her wanted to.

She looked back.

He was still watching her.

Chapter Two

What Was Left Behind

That night, Sofia sat at her kitchen table with a glass of wine she wasn't really drinking and thought about all the things she'd packed into boxes ten years ago and driven to her sister's storage unit because she couldn't throw them away but couldn't look at them either.

A concert ticket from their first real date. A photograph — them on a roof somewhere in Barcelona, squinting into the sun, laughing at something neither of them could probably remember now. A card he'd written her for their second anniversary. She still had it memorized: *I know we're still figuring everything out. I know some days are hard. But I'd rather figure it out with you than have everything solved without you. — M.*

She'd left anyway.

Her reasons had been real. She believed that then and she believed it now. She'd been twenty-six and terrified of being defined entirely by another person, even a person she loved more than she'd loved anything. She'd needed to know who she was when she wasn't Marcus's Sofia. She'd needed space to find that out.

She'd found it. She had a career she was proud of, a life she'd built with intention, friends who knew her well and loved her clearly. She was, by any reasonable measure, a woman who had her life together.

But she still had the card.

She poured the wine down the drain, rinsed the glass, and went to bed. She lay awake for a long time listening to the city outside her window and trying to remember all the reasons she'd made the choices she'd made.

By the time she fell asleep, she was no longer entirely certain she remembered them all.

Chapter Three

Coffee and Complicated Things

He texted her the next day. She didn't know how he still had her number, or why she hadn't blocked it in ten years — an oversight that felt, in retrospect, suspiciously deliberate.

*I know this is strange. Would you want to catch up sometime? Just coffee. No agenda.*

She stared at the message for four minutes. She knew because she timed it.

*Just coffee* was doing a lot of work in that sentence. Just coffee between two people with their history wasn't *just* anything. Just coffee was the first step in a sequence of events that her entire rational mind was warning her away from.

She texted back: *Sunday at 10. Blue Bottle on Morrison.*

He was already there when she arrived, which meant he'd been early, which meant he'd been nervous, which meant she hadn't been the only one lying awake thinking about things she'd thought she'd settled.

They talked for three hours. About his mother, and her work, and the city and how much it had changed and how much it hadn't. About his years in Seattle, her years of mostly staying put. They talked around the ten-year gap the way you talk around a hole in the floor — carefully, aware of the edges, never quite looking directly down.

"Are you seeing anyone?" he finally asked.

"No. You?"

"No. Not for a while."

She looked out the window at the street. "I have thought about you," she said, because it was Sunday morning and she was tired of carrying things alone. "I want you to know that. I never — I didn't just close a door and forget you."

He was quiet for a moment. "I know," he said. "I know you didn't."

Outside, the autumn light fell gold on the sidewalk, and neither of them reached for the check for a very long time.

Chapter Four

Learning Each Other Again

It started slowly, the way things that matter usually do.

They had coffee again the following Sunday, and then dinner the Sunday after that — a low-key Italian place on the east side where the tables were too small and the candles made everything look cinematic. They talked about books they'd read, trips they wanted to take. They talked about their parents, their fears, the ways they'd changed. Marcus had become quieter. Sofia had become more direct. They were both, in some ways, better versions of what they'd been.

But they were also different people now, and she wasn't sure yet whether those differences were things they could build around or things that would open up like fault lines the moment any real weight was put on them.

"What are we doing?" she asked him, four weeks in, sitting on a bench in Laurelhurst Park while the last of the October leaves came down.

"I think we're figuring that out," he said.

"I don't want to hurt you again." She said it simply, because it was the truest thing she could say.

He turned to look at her. "I'm a grown man, Sofia. I can handle the risk."

"That's not—" She stopped. "I'm trying to be responsible about this."

"I know. And I love that about you. I always loved that about you." He paused. "But responsible isn't the same as safe, and safe isn't always the same as right."

She thought about that for a long time, watching a duck paddle through the gray surface of the pond.

"I still have your card," she said finally. "The second anniversary one."

Something changed in his expression — something softened and also cracked a little, like ice at the edges of a lake in early spring.

"Sofia," he said.

"Don't say anything," she said. "Not yet. I just wanted you to know."

Chapter Five

The Last Kiss

November came, and with it, a cold that the city wore like a familiar coat. Sofia and Marcus had fallen into something she couldn't name yet — not quite relationship, not quite friendship, not quite the thing they'd been before. Something new. Something built on the bones of something old, which was both more fragile and more solid than anything entirely new could have been.

She was at his apartment for the first time on a Thursday evening, ostensibly to drop off a book she'd borrowed and meant to return. The apartment was warm and smelled like coffee and the old paperbacks he'd always collected. There was a blanket on the couch in a particular disarray that told her he'd been reading there before she arrived.

He took the book and set it on the counter and turned to her, and they stood in the kitchen looking at each other in the way they'd been looking at each other since the farmers market, which was to say with great intensity and very little resolution.

"You don't have to stay," he said.

"I know," she said. "I want to."

The first time they kissed again, it felt like both an ending and a beginning — like the final page of one book that is also the first page of the next, and you can't quite tell where one story stops and the other starts. It was tentative at first, careful, the way you'd touch something you weren't sure was still there.

It was still there.

Everything they'd built and lost and left behind and carried — it was still there, and it was also completely different, and it was also, finally, asking for a chance to be something new.

That night, lying in the dark with his hand in hers and the city going quiet outside, Sofia thought: *This is what it is to be brave.* Not the absence of fear. Not certainty about outcomes. Just the willingness to begin again, even knowing what it cost last time.

She had always been, in the end, braver than she gave herself credit for.

"Marcus," she said softly.

"Yeah?" Half-asleep, warm.

"I'm glad you still had my number."

He squeezed her hand. "I'm glad you still had the card."

Outside, the November rain started quietly, and they let it.
