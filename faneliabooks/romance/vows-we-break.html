Vows We Break

by Clara Vance

When love isn't enough, what is?

Chapter One

The Silence Between Us

Twelve years of marriage and Camille could tell, now, the exact texture of Marcus's unhappiness by the way he set his keys on the kitchen counter when he got home. A soft placement meant a neutral day. A hard placement — the keys hitting marble with a small crack of sound — meant she should ask no questions for at least an hour.

Tonight it was the hard placement. She did not ask.

They ate dinner with the television on because that had become the third person in their marriage, the one who filled all the silences so neither of them had to. She'd read an article once — in a dentist's waiting room, in a different life — that said couples who eat in front of the TV are avoiding each other. She'd thought, reading it: *not us.* She thought something different now.

"How was the Henley presentation?" she asked, during commercials.

"Fine." He didn't look up from his plate.

"They liked it?"

"They said they'd be in touch." He reached for the remote. "There's something on at eight I was going to catch."

She cleared the table. She loaded the dishwasher. She went to the bathroom and sat on the edge of the tub and breathed for a minute, which was something she'd started doing — finding small rooms to be alone in, small moments of air. She wasn't sad, exactly. She was tired. She was tired of the keys and the TV and the silences that had weight in them now.

She loved him. She was almost certain she still loved him. But love, she was learning, could coexist with a very specific kind of grief.

Chapter Two

The Things We Don't Say

She brought it up on a Saturday. She'd been planning to for two weeks and then talked herself out of it twice and then decided, standing at the kitchen window watching him rake leaves in the backyard, that there was no good time and never would be.

"I think we need to talk," she said, when he came in.

He looked at her over his glass of water. He knew. She could tell by the particular stillness in him that he'd been waiting for this too. Maybe longer than she had.

They sat at the kitchen table without turning the TV on. It felt radical. The quiet was enormous.

"I think we've gotten lost," she said. "I don't mean — I'm not saying—" She started again. "I miss you. That's what I'm trying to say. You're right here and I miss you."

He was quiet for a long moment. "I know."

"That's it? You know?"

"What do you want me to say, Camille?" Not unkind. Just tired, and tired in the same way she was, and she realized with a dull shock that he'd been as lonely as she had. They'd been separately lonely right next to each other.

"I want to know if we can fix it," she said. "I want to know if you want to."

He looked at her for a long time. Outside, the afternoon was gray and still, and the leaves he'd raked had blown slightly undone.

"I want to want to," he said.

It wasn't yes. It wasn't no. It was the most honest thing he'd said to her in a year, and she held it carefully, because honest things were fragile and she'd learned that, once broken, they didn't always mend the same way.

Chapter Three

What Twelve Years Knows

They went to couples counseling. The therapist was a small woman with excellent posture and a talent for asking questions that felt simple and weren't. Her name was Dr. Chen and she charged by the hour and in the first session she asked them each what they loved about the other, which felt performative and wasn't.

Marcus had said, after a pause: "She remembers things. She remembers everything about people — what they told her once two years ago, what they're worried about. She makes people feel like they matter."

He hadn't looked at her when he said it. He'd looked at his hands. Camille had felt something move in her chest.

She'd said: "He shows up. No matter what — when my father was sick, when I lost the Aldrich contract and I was a mess for a month — he's always just there. He doesn't always know what to say but he stays."

They'd both been surprised by what the other said, which told her something. They'd been so deep in the silence that they'd forgotten the reasons underneath it.

Dr. Chen had nodded, made a note, and said: "What happened to the people who said those things about each other?"

Neither of them had an answer right then. But they both went home thinking about it, and for the first time in months, Camille felt something that wasn't grief — something closer to curiosity, which was, she was learning, the beginning of most things that mattered.

Chapter Four

Learning to Fight

The fighting was new. Or, more accurately, the real fighting was new — the kind that had actual content in it, actual stakes. For years they'd edged around conflict the way you'd edge around furniture in a dark room. Now they were turning on lights and bumping into things and sometimes it hurt.

But Dr. Chen had said something in their fourth session that stayed with both of them: "Couples who never fight aren't peaceful. They're afraid. The fight isn't the problem. The fight is often proof that both people still care what happens."

Camille cared what happened. She discovered this conclusively on a Wednesday evening when Marcus said something dismissive about her work, a casual thing, the kind of thing he might have said a hundred times, and she put her fork down and said, "That's not okay," and then said exactly what wasn't okay about it in plain, specific language.

He'd gone quiet with the particular quality of someone recalibrating.

"You're right," he said, after a moment. "I'm sorry. That was dismissive."

She'd blinked. In twelve years he had not, she realized, said those words in quite that order quite that directly. She'd always gotten the apologies that were apologies for her feelings rather than for the action. This was different.

"Thank you," she said.

They'd looked at each other across the table and something had shifted slightly, like furniture moved a few inches to let the light in.

It wasn't fixed. It wasn't anywhere close to fixed. But for the first time in a long time, Camille thought it might not be unfixable.

Chapter Five

A Different Kind of Vow

Spring came, and with it, a fragile and effortful tenderness. Nothing was easy and nothing was certain and some nights were still quiet in the wrong ways. But there were also mornings now when Marcus made coffee and set a mug by her side of the desk without being asked, knowing she'd come to it; evenings when they talked past the end of the television program because the conversation had turned into something they didn't want to stop.

Dr. Chen had a word for what they were doing. She called it re-choosing — the active, daily decision to choose this person, this life, over the easier alternative of drift. It was less romantic than the first choosing, which had the momentum of infatuation behind it. This was something quieter and more deliberate.

On their anniversary, Marcus made a reservation at the restaurant where they'd had their third date — a place so small it had only seven tables, with a menu that changed monthly and a proprietor who remembered regulars. It had been years since they'd been.

"I remember this place," she said, when they sat down.

"I was hoping you would."

She looked at him across the table — this man she'd been married to for twelve years, who she'd been lost with and was slowly finding again — and she thought about vows. The ones they'd made in the church with their families watching, the general and public ones. And the ones that had turned out to matter more: the private and specific and daily ones, the ones that didn't have words, the choice made quietly every morning to stay.

"Thank you for staying," she said.

He reached across the table. "Thank you for asking me to."

Outside, the spring evening was soft and ordinary and theirs. She held his hand and thought: this, too, is a kind of love. The chosen, deliberate, imperfect kind.

Maybe the most real kind of all.
