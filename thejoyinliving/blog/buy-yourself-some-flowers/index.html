Buy Yourself Some Flowers 🌸

A gentle reminder about the importance of self-compassion and small acts of self-care.

CS
Christina Soddano, LCSWLicensed Clinical Social Worker · Sleepy Hollow, NY

When was the last time you did something kind for yourself — not as a reward, not after achieving something, just because you deserved it?

For many of us, self-care feels like something we have to earn. We put everyone else first — our partners, our children, our clients, our colleagues — and by the time we get to ourselves, there's nothing left. And even then, we often feel guilty for wanting more.

This is not a personal failing. It's a pattern — one that therapy can help you understand, challenge, and change.

What Self-Compassion Actually Means

Self-compassion is not self-indulgence or selfishness. Researcher Kristin Neff, who has spent decades studying this, defines self-compassion as treating yourself with the same kindness, warmth, and understanding that you would offer a good friend.

Think about that. If your best friend was exhausted, grieving, struggling — what would you say to them? Would you tell them they need to do more, be more, achieve more before they deserve rest? Of course not. You'd hold space for them. You'd remind them they're enough.

🌸 You deserve the same compassion you so freely give to others. That's not a luxury — it's a mental health necessity.

Small Acts, Real Impact

Self-care doesn't have to be a spa weekend or a two-week vacation (though if you can, those are wonderful). Research shows that small, intentional acts of self-care — consistently practiced — have a meaningful positive impact on mental health, stress levels, and emotional resilience.

  • Buy yourself flowers — literally. Studies show that having flowers in your space improves mood.
  • Take your lunch break away from your desk, even for 15 minutes
  • Read something just because it brings you joy — not because it's educational or productive
  • Call a friend you haven't spoken to in a while
  • Say no to something that drains you, without apologizing
  • Make the meal you love, not the one that's fastest

When Self-Care Feels Impossible

If the idea of taking care of yourself feels impossible — through depression, trauma, overwhelm, or a simple lack of energy — that's important information. It often signals that what you need goes beyond bubble baths and walks in the park.

Therapy is a form of deep self-care. It's making weekly time to attend to your inner world with professional support. Many clients tell us that starting therapy was the first time in years they did something truly for themselves.

A Note on Cultural Expectations

For many of our clients — particularly women, immigrants, and people from collectivist cultural backgrounds — self-care carries additional weight. The cultural messages are strong: put others first, don't be a burden, keep going no matter what.

We honor these values. And we also believe that you can love and care for your family, your community, and yourself at the same time. These are not in conflict. In fact, when you fill your own cup, you have more — genuinely more — to give to the people you love.

Take the First Step for Yourself
